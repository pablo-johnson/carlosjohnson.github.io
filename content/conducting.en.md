Moving from the concertmaster's desk to the podium is a natural development in the careers of many orchestral leaders — Lorin Maazel, Neville Marriner and Tibor Varga himself all made it — but it does not happen by itself. Carlos Johnson began that path formally in 2017, after decades observing the inner mechanics of an orchestra from the best seat available.

## Training

Rather than teaching himself, he became a student of the German conductor **Ludwig Pflanz**, a familiar and respected figure in Lübeck and a regular guest at the Theater Lübeck, in opera productions as well as symphonic and contemporary repertoire.

From him he absorbed the gestural vocabulary of the German school: how to convey ideas of tempo, phrasing and colour to more than a hundred musicians, wordlessly and in real time, which until then he had passed on through the movement of his own bow.

## On the podium

Since then he has been invited to conduct symphonic ensembles in Europe and the Americas.

The most visible moment came in **May 2024**, when he took on the double role of conductor and solo violinist with the **National Symphony Orchestra of Colombia**, in the format known as *play/direct*: leading from the instrument, without a baton.

That format is a demanding test of control and authority. It requires having internalised the full score — the entries of every wind, brass and percussion part — and holding the whole ensemble together while playing the solo line. The Universidad Nacional de Música in Peru issued a public commendation for the performance.

## Music and new audiences

His teaching instinct does not stop at the classroom. In Lübeck he takes part in symphonic education projects aimed at building audiences, in cooperation with the city's state museums.

Chief among them are the *Klangbilderkonzerte*, concerts that interweave music, literature and the visual arts. One of them set a reading of *Im Krebsgang* — the novella by the Nobel laureate Günter Grass — against works by Bach and Ravel.

## What the concertmaster brings to the podium

Spending more than two decades translating a conductor's gestures into concrete technical instruction leaves a mark that is hard to acquire any other way: he knows exactly what a section player needs to hear in order to understand what is being asked, and when a gesture is saying less than it thinks it is.

It is the same idea that underpins his teaching and his playing: the performer is in the service of the work, and so is the conductor.
